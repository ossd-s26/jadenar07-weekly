Something I'd kind of taken for granted up to this point was the financial ask of Open Source. I kept going with the assumption that when a big company contributes to an open source project, it's because they gain the use of that software and it would ultimately be straightforwardly good for the project. What could go wrong? YOu get more resources, more engineers, more momentum. But the business side of things definitely complicates that in ways I hadn't really thought about before.

The commodity vs differentiation framework is what caught my attention most. We've talked a lot in this class about how companies use and contribute to open source but we never really dived deeply into the why. It was interesting to hear about companies like Toyota and Mercedes collaborating on automotive grade linux despite competing for the same car buyers. It sounds contradictory after first but it's far from. For these companies, they quickly come to the realization that the most rational thing is to accept that neither of them wins customers because of their Bluetooth or internal software. That there's other things for them to focus on and it's better to have that aspect cohesive and consistent, while they make other features the attraction. The open source collaboration happens exactly where competition would be pointless or less relevant. Make one good version and save engineering resources for the stuff that actually matters to their market position.

One thing that's really drawn my attention is thinking about where that line sits and who gets to draw it. Because when a company like Goldman Sachs open-sources Legend through FINOS, they're not just being generous,the standardization around their data model benefits Goldman heavily. They're large enough that if the industry adopts their definitions, that works in their favor. The contribution is real and useful, but the motivation is pretty transparently strategic. Which made me wonder, does that dynamic quietly shape which parts of a project get resources and attention, and which don't?

Something far from new to open source is how much influence a highly active contributor can accumulate just through sheer commit volume. When that contributor is a corporation with full-time engineers dedicated to a project, the power asymmetry with volunteer maintainers becomes pretty big. That contributor's opinion starts to hold more value, their work given more recognition and priorty. The same happens when companies become that contributor. They can bring the volume but also even funding. Funding means influence. It's fun thinking about the layers to the situation and how complex Open Source can really get
